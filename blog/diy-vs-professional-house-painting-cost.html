DIY vs Professional House Painting: What It Actually Costs You

By Dan Eaton • August 16, 2026 • 9 min read

Repainting a home is a significant job, and it’s natural to weigh up whether to tackle it yourself or bring in a professional. The upfront quote for professional painting can look like the more expensive option at first glance — but “the actual cost” of a paint job is about more than just the number on a quote. It includes your time, the tools and materials you’ll need to buy, the risk of mistakes, and how long the finished job actually lasts.

We’re obviously not a neutral party here — we’re professional painters. But we’ve also seen plenty of DIY jobs, both the ones that turned out well and the ones that didn’t, and there are some genuinely useful things to weigh up before you decide.

What a DIY Paint Job Actually Costs

The obvious costs are paint, primer, brushes, rollers, drop sheets, masking tape, sandpaper, and possibly ladders or scaffolding for a two-storey home. Add those up and it’s often more than people expect, particularly if you need to buy tools you don’t already own.

The less obvious cost is time. A full exterior repaint on an average family home, done properly — including preparation — is not a weekend job. Preparation alone (cleaning, scraping, sanding, filling, priming) typically takes as long as the painting itself, often longer, and it’s the step that gets rushed or skipped when people are doing it themselves around work and family commitments.

What Professional Painting Costs

A professional quote reflects labour, materials, insurance, and the equipment needed to do the job properly and safely, including height access where required. It will generally be more than the cost of materials alone for a DIY job — that’s the trade-off for someone else doing the work, doing it efficiently, and standing behind the result.

Every home is different in terms of size, condition, and complexity, which is why we quote each job individually rather than working off a flat rate. If you want real numbers for your specific property, a free quote is the most accurate way to get them — but the rest of this article is about the costs that don’t show up on either quote.

The Hidden Cost of Getting It Wrong

This is where the real cost difference often shows up. Preparation is where most paint jobs succeed or fail — not the paint itself, not the brand, not even the technique of applying it. If surfaces aren’t properly cleaned, sanded, primed, and any repairs made before the topcoats go on, the paint job will fail early, regardless of how good the paint is.

A DIY job that fails after two or three years, rather than lasting the seven to ten years a quality job should, effectively costs more than doing it properly the first time — you’ve paid for materials and your own time twice, plus whatever repairs are needed for any damage that occurred while the paint was failing.

Time: The Cost People Forget to Count

If you value your weekends, or you’re balancing a repaint around work and family life, it’s worth being honest about how many weekends a full exterior job will actually take when you’re not doing it full-time. What a professional crew completes in a few days can easily stretch across several weekends — sometimes months — when it’s squeezed in around everything else. That’s not a criticism of DIY, just a real cost that’s easy to underestimate when you’re only looking at the price of paint.

Safety and Access

Exterior painting often involves ladders, scaffolding, or working at height — particularly on two-storey homes, or homes with steep rooflines. This is one area where the cost comparison isn’t really about money at all. Professional painters have the right equipment and experience to work safely at height; for most homeowners, that’s a genuine risk worth factoring into the decision, not just a line item.

Resale and Presentation Value

A well-executed paint job noticeably lifts a home’s kerb appeal and can support a stronger sale price, particularly in a market like the Southern Highlands where presentation matters. A DIY job that looks slightly uneven, or where the prep work shows through in a few years, doesn’t deliver the same value — even though the upfront cost was lower.

Insurance and Liability

This is a cost that’s easy to overlook entirely. A licensed, insured painter carries public liability cover and, for their employees, workers compensation — so if something goes wrong on the job, whether that’s damage to your property or an injury on site, there’s cover in place. If you’re painting your own home and something goes wrong — a fall from a ladder, damage to a neighbour’s property, a mishap with equipment — you’re generally on your own, and depending on the circumstances, your home and contents insurance may not cover it either.

It’s worth checking your own policy if you’re considering a DIY job at height, and factoring that risk into your overall comparison, not just the cost of paint and brushes.

Equipment You Might Not Already Own

Beyond the obvious brushes and rollers, a proper exterior job often needs equipment most homeowners don’t have on hand — extension ladders rated for the height involved, drop sheets, quality masking products, pressure washing equipment for surface preparation, and sanding tools for dealing with failing paint. Buying or hiring all of this adds to the real cost of a DIY job, and it’s equipment you may only use once, which changes the value equation compared to a professional crew who already own and maintain it.

When DIY Makes Sense

To be fair, DIY isn’t the wrong choice for everyone or every job. A single-storey home with straightforward access, an owner with genuine painting experience and the time to do proper preparation, and a realistic understanding of the work involved — that combination can produce a perfectly good result. Smaller jobs, like a single room or a garage floor, are also more forgiving for a first-time DIY attempt than a full exterior repaint.

The Cost of Getting It Wrong Twice

One scenario worth thinking through: a DIY exterior job that fails within a couple of years, prompting the homeowner to then bring in a professional to fix it properly. At that point, you’ve paid for materials and your own time on the original attempt, then paid for a professional repaint on top — often at a higher cost than if a professional had done the job properly the first time, since fixing a failing DIY job can require more preparation work than starting fresh. This is a common enough pattern that it’s worth factoring into your decision upfront, rather than after the fact.

Getting an Honest Comparison

The best way to actually compare the two options for your home is to get a real quote and weigh it against a genuine estimate of your own time, tools, and the risk of getting the prep wrong. We’re happy to give you that number, and an honest opinion on whether your particular job is a reasonable DIY candidate or one where the preparation work makes professional painting the better value.
