Best Exterior Paint Colours for Heritage Homes in Bowral & Berrima

By Dan Eaton • August 6, 2026 • 9 min read

If you own one of the Southern Highlands’ heritage homes — a Georgian cottage in Berrima, a period residence in Bowral, or a character weatherboard in Sutton Forest — choosing an exterior colour scheme is about more than personal taste. It’s about respecting the architecture, the era the home was built in, and the streetscape it sits within.

We’ve painted heritage and character homes right across the Highlands, and one thing is consistent: the right colour scheme doesn’t just look good, it makes the home’s architectural details — the trims, the mouldings, the joinery — actually visible and legible, instead of flattening everything into one tone.

Why Heritage Homes Need a Different Approach

A modern home can often carry bold, contemporary colour choices without any issue. A heritage home is different — it was designed with a particular aesthetic language in mind, and the colours chosen at the time (or historically appropriate to the era) were part of that design. Georgian-era homes, common around Berrima, favoured more restrained, muted palettes. Victorian and Federation-era homes, more common through Bowral and Burradoo, often had richer, more varied colour schemes with multiple tones picking out different architectural elements.

Getting this wrong doesn’t just look slightly off — on a genuinely heritage-listed property, it can also run into council heritage overlay requirements, which is worth checking before you commit to a scheme if your property falls under one.

Classic Heritage-Appropriate Colour Palettes

While every home is different, there are a few colour families that consistently work well on Highlands heritage and character homes:

  • Warm off-whites and creams — a classic base colour for Georgian and early Victorian homes, still one of the most popular choices today
  • Heritage greens — deep, muted greens for doors, trims and window frames, a genuine period colour that reads as timeless rather than dated
  • Deep charcoal and near-black — increasingly popular for weatherboard and iron roofing on character homes, giving a striking contemporary edge while still suiting older architecture
  • Soft heritage blues and greys — particularly well suited to homes with a lot of exposed timber trim or fretwork
  • Ochre and warm stone tones — complement sandstone and brick detailing common on some of the older Berrima properties

Choosing a Base Colour vs. Trim Colours

Most heritage colour schemes work on a two or three-colour system: a dominant body colour, a contrasting trim colour for window frames, fascia and barge boards, and often a third accent colour for the front door. Getting the balance right between these is what makes a heritage home’s architectural detail stand out, rather than disappear.

A common mistake is choosing a trim colour that’s too close in tone to the body colour — from a distance, all that carefully carved detailing just vanishes into a flat wall of colour. A trim colour with genuine contrast, even if subtle, does far more to show off the home’s character.

Working With, Not Against, the Original Materials

Many Highlands heritage homes combine several materials — sandstone footings, brick, weatherboard, iron roofing, timber joinery — and a good colour scheme considers all of them together, not just the walls in isolation. Sandstone and exposed brick, in particular, already carry a lot of warm tone, and the paint colours chosen for the timber and iron elements need to sit comfortably alongside that rather than clash with it.

Modern Touches Without Losing Character

It’s entirely possible to give a heritage home a fresher, more contemporary feel without abandoning its character — deep charcoal roofing paired with a warm off-white body colour is a good example, a combination that reads as both period-appropriate and current. The key is making one deliberate, well-considered choice rather than trying to force too many competing ideas into the one scheme.

A Practical Process for Choosing Your Colours

When we work through a colour consultation on a heritage or character home, we generally recommend:

  1. Look at what’s worked well on comparable homes nearby — the Highlands has no shortage of well-executed heritage colour schemes to draw inspiration from
  2. Test genuine sample patches on the actual home, in different light, before committing — a colour swatch under shop lighting looks very different on a north-facing weatherboard wall at 4pm
  3. Consider the roof colour as part of the whole scheme, not an afterthought decided separately
  4. Check whether your property falls under a heritage overlay, and what constraints that puts on colour choice
  5. Get an experienced opinion — after years of painting Highlands homes, we can usually tell fairly quickly what will and won’t work on a given property

Sheen and Finish Matter As Much As Colour

It’s easy to focus entirely on the colour and forget that the finish — matte, low-sheen, satin or gloss — changes how a colour actually reads on a heritage home. Historically, exterior joinery like doors, window frames and fretwork were often finished in a higher gloss than the main body of the house, which was typically flatter. That contrast in sheen, as much as the contrast in colour, is part of what gives a heritage colour scheme its depth.

A very high-gloss finish across an entire weatherboard exterior can look distinctly modern and out of place on an older home, while too flat a finish on trim details can make them look chalky and washed out rather than crisp. Getting this balance right is a small detail that makes a real difference to the finished result.

Common Heritage Colour Mistakes to Avoid

A few missteps come up again and again on heritage and character homes:

  • Choosing colours that are historically accurate for a completely different era or region — heritage-appropriate doesn’t mean any old colour, it means colours suited to that specific home’s architectural style
  • Painting over original textured or timber-grain surfaces with a flat, uniform finish that removes character rather than enhancing it
  • Ignoring the roof colour when planning the scheme, then finding it clashes badly with the newly painted walls and trim
  • Going too bold on a home where the surrounding streetscape is uniformly restrained, which can look jarring rather than characterful
  • Skipping a test patch and committing to a full repaint based on a small paint chip or a screen colour swatch, which rarely looks the same at full scale in natural light

Getting It Right the First Time

A full exterior repaint is a significant investment, and getting the colour scheme wrong is an expensive mistake to live with for years. We offer colour consultation as part of our quoting process for exactly this reason — helping you land on a scheme that suits your home’s architecture, its era, and the character of its street, before a drop of paint goes on.

It’s also worth remembering that a colour scheme doesn’t need to be decided in isolation from the rest of the project. If you’re repainting a heritage home, it’s a good moment to also address any timber repairs, failing render, or roof condition issues at the same time, so the whole exterior is brought up to the same standard together, rather than having a beautifully painted home let down by an unaddressed roof or a patch of unrepaired trim.
