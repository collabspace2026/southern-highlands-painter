How Often Should You Repaint a Weatherboard Home in the Southern Highlands?

By Dan Eaton • August 1, 2026 • 8 min read

If you own a weatherboard home in the Southern Highlands, you’ve probably wondered at some point whether it’s time to repaint — or whether that faded, slightly chalky finish still has a few years left in it. It’s one of the most common questions we get asked, and the honest answer is: it depends on your specific home, its aspect, and how it’s been maintained. But there are clear signs to look for, and a rough timeline that holds true for most weatherboard homes in this climate.

After 25 years painting homes across Bowral, Bundanoon, Moss Vale and the surrounding villages, we’ve seen weatherboard paint jobs that have held up beautifully for over a decade, and others that started failing within three or four years. The difference almost always comes down to preparation, product choice, and the specific conditions the home is exposed to.

Why Weatherboard Behaves Differently to Brick or Render

Weatherboard is timber, and timber moves. It expands and contracts with temperature and humidity, which puts more stress on a paint film than a solid masonry surface does. If the timber isn’t properly primed and sealed before the topcoats go on, moisture can get in behind the paint — through joins, nail holes, or the end grain at the bottom of each board — and that’s where you start to see cracking, peeling and, eventually, rot.

This is also why weatherboard homes generally need repainting more often than brick or rendered homes. A quality paint job on brick might last 12–15 years; on weatherboard, even a very good paint job is usually doing well to get past 10.

The Southern Highlands Climate Factor

The Highlands climate adds its own pressure on top of that. Cooler, wetter winters than most of the Sydney basin mean more moisture sitting on exterior surfaces for longer, and a shorter window each year where conditions are genuinely ideal for painting. Frost in the colder months, strong UV in summer, and the humidity that comes with the elevation all take a toll on a paint film over time.

Homes on more exposed sites — open to prevailing weather, or without much tree cover — tend to show wear faster than a home tucked into a more sheltered garden. South and west-facing walls typically fade and chalk faster than north-facing ones too, simply from more direct sun exposure through the afternoon.

Signs Your Weatherboard Needs Repainting

Rather than working purely off a calendar, it’s worth doing a walk-around of your home once or twice a year and checking for these signs:

  • Chalking — a powdery residue on the surface that comes off on your hand when you wipe it
  • Fading or uneven colour, especially on sun-exposed walls
  • Cracking or flaking paint, particularly around joins and board edges
  • Small gaps opening up where boards meet, or around window and door frames
  • Soft or spongy spots in the timber, which can indicate moisture has gotten in
  • Peeling paint that lifts away in sheets rather than just fine flaking

Any one of these on its own might just mean a touch-up is in order. Several of them together, especially cracking combined with soft timber, usually means it’s time for a proper repaint before the underlying timber suffers more damage.

So, How Often Should You Actually Repaint?

As a general guide for weatherboard homes in this climate, most well-prepared and quality-painted exteriors will need a full repaint somewhere in the 7–10 year range. Homes on more exposed sites, or ones that were painted without thorough preparation the first time around, can start showing real wear closer to the 5–7 year mark. On the other end, a home that’s well sheltered, properly maintained, and was painted with premium products and proper surface prep can sometimes stretch out past 10 years before it needs a full repaint.

It’s worth noting this is different from a simple refresh coat, which some homeowners choose to do every few years on the most exposed walls to keep ahead of fading, without doing the full home. That can be a sensible way to extend the interval between full repaints, provided the touch-up work is done properly and matched well.

What Happens If You Leave It Too Long

The risk with pushing a repaint out too far isn’t just cosmetic. Once paint starts failing and moisture gets into exposed timber, you’re no longer just looking at a paint job — you’re looking at timber repairs first. Rotted board sections need to be replaced, which adds cost and time to the project, and if it’s left long enough, structural elements like fascia and eaves can be affected too.

Catching it early is almost always cheaper in the long run. A repaint on sound, well-prepared timber is a relatively contained job. A repaint that has to start with cutting out and replacing damaged boards is a bigger undertaking, and one that’s entirely avoidable with timely maintenance.

How to Extend the Life of Your Paint Job

A few things make a real difference to how long a weatherboard paint job lasts:

  • Thorough surface preparation before painting — this is where most paint jobs succeed or fail, far more than the paint brand used
  • Quality, weather-resistant paint products suited to the Southern Highlands climate, not just whatever’s cheapest
  • Proper priming of bare or repaired timber before topcoats go on
  • Keeping gutters clear and downpipes working, so water isn’t running down or pooling against exterior walls
  • Trimming back vegetation that’s in constant contact with the exterior, which traps moisture against the timber
  • A light wash of the exterior once or twice a year to remove grime and mould before it builds up

DIY or Professional?

Repainting weatherboard yourself is possible, and some homeowners do take it on. But the preparation work — scraping back failing paint, sanding, priming bare timber, and making sure every board and join is properly sealed — is time-consuming and easy to underdo when you’re not doing it every day. A rushed prep job is the single biggest reason a DIY repaint fails early, often within just a few years.

A professional exterior repaint costs more upfront, but if it means the difference between a paint job that lasts five years and one that lasts ten, it’s usually the better value over time — especially once you factor in your own time and the risk of getting the prep wrong on a home you’ll be looking at every day.
